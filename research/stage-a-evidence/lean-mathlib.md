## system

Lean 4 (theorem prover + general-purpose programming language, leanprover/lean4) and mathlib4 (the community mathematical library, leanprover-community/mathlib4), plus the immediate ecosystem relevant to the proposed product: SciLean (scientific computing), PhysLean/Physlib (physics), lean4export/leanchecker/lean4lean (proof export and external checking), all stewarded since July 2023 by the Lean FRO nonprofit.

## overlap

Mechanism-level overlap is substantial on the logic/proof axis. (1) Typed declarative KR: Lean's dependent type theory is a strictly stronger typing discipline than "predicate calls with named places" — every statement is a term whose type is checked at elaboration; mathlib's typeclass hierarchy (Group, MeasureSpace, NormedField, ...) is exactly a curated, fail-closed scientific vocabulary at 135,859 definitions / 285,767 theorems. (2) Fail-closed name resolution: an undeclared identifier, a missing typeclass instance, or a universe error is a compile error, never a default — the same design commitment the product makes, proven viable at scale. (3) Exact inference with proof objects: the kernel checks every proof down to a small trusted core; this is the strongest existing implementation of the product's "proved" verdict, including independent re-checking (leanchecker replays environments through the kernel; lean4lean is a near-independent kernel reimplementation that has found real kernel bugs; lean4export dumps kernel-level declarations as NDJSON for external tools). (4) Deterministic, reproducible builds: elan pins the toolchain per-project (lean-toolchain file), lake pins dependency revisions, .olean artifacts are content-addressed — a working provenance/replay story for the *logical* fragment. (5) Verified symbolic/numeric procedures: norm_num proves numeric (in)equalities by reflection over binary numerals with algebraic lemmas; ring, linarith, polyrith, and the improving grind/lia tactics are verified symbolic math; polyrith even delegates to an external CAS and re-certifies the certificate — a miniature of the product's "orchestrate external solvers, trust only checked results" pattern. (6) Domain coverage on the mathematics quadrant: calculus, linear algebra, measure-theoretic probability, ODE existence theory, functional analysis are formalized; PhysLean/Physlib (710 stars, 7 maintainers) extends into physics including a dimensional-analysis framework (dimensions as an abelian group, SI units, Buckingham Pi, arXiv:2509.13142).

## insufficiency

Missing mechanisms, not ambitions. (1) No computation from the formalization: mathlib's ℝ is noncomputable; kernel evaluation is "impossible on noncomputable types such as ℝ" (mathlib community docs); Picard-Lindelöf is existence-only with `noncomputable` definitions — there is no path from a formalized ODE to a numeric trajectory. The bridge project, SciLean, self-describes as an early-stage proof of concept, not production. (2) No solver orchestration layer: no typed dispatch to external numeric solvers, no seeds/tolerances/solver-identity receipts, no batch-job model; external results can only enter as axioms or via native_decide (which enlarges the TCB to the compiler). (3) No evidence/uncertainty types: everything is a theorem or nothing; there is no representation of measurement, parameter, or structural uncertainty, hence no "supported/refuted/conflicting/out_of_regime" verdicts — Lean's result taxonomy is exactly {kernel accepts, error}, with no disposition axis. (4) No units in the core: mathlib has no quantity/unit types; dimensional analysis lives in a 2025 external formalization and PhysLean, not in the standard library. (5) Latency/cost model: mathlib builds are heavy, elaboration of a file is seconds-to-minutes, and proof authorship runs at a de Bruijn factor of ~4-10 and ~150-250 lines/day for experts (0.5-50 person-months per paper) — incompatible with a working scientist asserting hundreds of empirical claims per day. (6) No claim-level provenance: provenance is git + toolchain pinning at repository granularity, not per-claim receipts with source/artifact hashes and replay instructions.

## narrowing_history

Lean did not narrow; it widened. First commit July 2013 (Leonardo de Moura, then Microsoft Research) as a theorem prover; Lean 3.0 Jan 2017; Lean 4.0 stable Sept 2023 rebuilt as a general-purpose programming language *and* prover; the Lean FRO (founded July 2023 under Convergent Research) explicitly targets three fronts — mathematics, software/hardware verification, and AI-assisted theorem proving (lean-lang.org/fro/about, accessed 2026-08-26). The one genuine contraction I recall — Lean 2's homotopy type theory mode dropped in Lean 3 — I did not verify in-session (evidence-incomplete). The relevant history lesson is instead the Lean 3→4 transition: a breaking rewrite that required a multi-year community port of mathlib; the ecosystem survived it because of centralized community infrastructure (Zulip, mathlib CI/Bors, the FRO), a luxury a solo-developer product will not have.

## adoption

Observable and strong for a proof assistant, niche relative to scientific-computing tools. Seen directly on 2026-08-26: lean4 8.9k stars, 41k commits, active release cadence (v4.33.0 on 2026-08-10, v4.33.1 on 2026-08-21); mathlib4 3.9k stars, 34,218 commits, 3.1k PRs, 772 contributors, 30+ maintainers, 285,767 theorems; Lean FRO funded by Sloan, Simons Foundation International, Alex Gerko, with recent corporate donations from Harmonic (Feb 2026) and Amazon (July 2026) — direct evidence that AI-for-math companies and AWS are invested. PhysLean 710 stars with a Comput. Phys. Commun. paper (2025). Adoption by working scientists for *computation* is essentially nil: SciLean (the scientific-computing entry point) is explicitly early-stage, and physics formalization is a research activity, not a lab workflow.

## lessons

Copy: (a) the trust architecture — small kernel, proof objects, independent re-checkers (leanchecker in-toolchain since v4.28.0; lean4lean as a second implementation that found real kernel bugs) and a plain-text export format (lean4export NDJSON) — this is the shape the product's proof/provenance receipts should take; (b) fail-closed elaboration with typeclass-carried vocabulary as the precedent that scientists' tolerance for compile errors can be earned if error messages name the missing instance; (c) verified reflection (norm_num) as the pattern for making exact arithmetic fast without trusting the evaluator; (d) toolchain/dependency pinning (elan + lake + content-addressed artifacts) for deterministic builds; (e) consider Lean as an export target: emitting Lean statements (even sorry-stubbed) for the product's "proved" fragment buys external checkability without building a kernel. Mistake to avoid: coupling *all* knowledge to full proof obligation. Lean's proof-cost wall (de Bruijn 4-10x, months per paper) and its computationally inert, noncomputable-by-default analysis are precisely why no working scientist runs simulations from mathlib; the product's supported/refuted/uncertainty verdicts and solver delegation are the mechanisms Lean lacks — keep them first-class rather than treating them as degraded proofs. Also avoid a Lean-3→4-style surface-language rewrite without a migration machine.

## cluster_answer

Stating vs proving: stating is cheap — a theorem statement elaborates in seconds once the vocabulary exists (and `sorry`/axioms let you assert without proof, tracked by the kernel via #print axioms); proving is the expense, at a de Bruijn factor of ~4-10 formal lines per informal line, ~150-250 lines/day for an expert, and estimates of 0.5-50 person-months per research paper. The practical boundary is therefore vocabulary-limited on the stating side (if mathlib lacks the definitions, you must build them first) and labor-limited on the proving side. Formalized vs computable: calculus (FTC, Fréchet derivatives, integrals), linear algebra (matrices, spectral theory), ODE existence (Picard-Lindelöf, verified in mathlib4 docs as existence-only with `noncomputable` Picard iterates), and measure-theoretic probability are formalized — but over noncomputable ℝ, so essentially all of it is computationally inert: kernel evaluation is impossible on ℝ, and computation happens only through verified reflection tactics (norm_num, decide, ring) for exact rational/integer arithmetic, or native_decide at the cost of trusting the compiler. There is no numeric ODE solving, no floating-point error-bounded simulation in mathlib; SciLean (AD, arrays, optimization) is the bridge attempt and is explicitly an early-stage proof of concept. Units/physics: none in mathlib core; PhysLean/Physlib is active (710 stars, CPC 2025 paper) and a 2025 formalization (arXiv:2509.13142) builds dimensions as an abelian group with SI units and the Buckingham Pi theorem; Terence Tao maintains a UnitsSystem.lean in his analysis repo — all external, none standard. Interop: lean4export emits kernel declarations as NDJSON; the export can be re-checked by external checkers — leanchecker (in the toolchain since v4.28.0, replacing the archived lean4checker) and lean4lean (a mostly-Lean reimplementation of the kernel with partial formal verification, which has caught real kernel bugs). There is no standard import from or export to other provers' logics; proof terms are checkable, not portable.

## largest_unverified_assumption

That mathlib's formalized analysis/probability is *uniformly* computationally inert — verified by spot-check (Picard-Lindelöf, noncomputable ℝ, norm_num docs) but not exhaustively; pockets of computable/decidable mathematics (finite combinatorics, exact linear algebra over ℚ, decidable props) exist, and I did not measure how large or usable that computable fragment is, nor did I verify the person-hour proof-cost figures beyond paper snippets.
