## system

Dakota (Sandia National Laboratories' optimization/UQ orchestration framework, C++, active since 1994, current release 6.24) together with the computational-science V&V/UQ programme: ASME V&V 10 (computational solid mechanics) and V&V 20 (CFD/heat transfer) standards, and the Oberkampf–Roy methodology codified in "Verification and Validation in Scientific Computing" (Cambridge, 2010).

## overlap

Dakota is the shipped instance of the product's orchestrator-plus-uncertainty half. Mechanism: the user writes a keyword-tree input deck with blocks for `method`, `variables`, `interface`, `responses`. The `interface` block declares a black-box contract over an arbitrary external simulation: with the fork interface, Dakota writes a parameters file, invokes a user-supplied `analysis_driver` script with the parameters/results filenames on its command line, the driver runs the simulation code, writes a results file, and Dakota parses the responses — exactly the product's "typed external solver behind a declared parameter/response interface," proven at national-lab HPC scale with asynchronous parallel job scheduling, file tagging/saving, and failure-capturing (retry/recover/continuation). Over that contract Dakota runs the product's intended analyses: forward UQ (LHS/MC sampling, polynomial chaos, stochastic collocation, reliability/MPP methods), global sensitivity (Sobol indices), Bayesian calibration, interval analysis, Dempster–Shafer evidence theory, and optimization. Crucially it already mechanizes the never-collapse doctrine: the variables block types aleatory inputs as probability distributions (normal, lognormal, weibull, histogram, …) and epistemic inputs as a distinct kind — continuous intervals, discrete ranges/sets, or interval sets with basic probability assignments — and mixed problems run as nested "second-order probability": an outer loop over epistemic realizations, an inner aleatory probabilistic loop, yielding interval-valued statistics / p-boxes / belief-plausibility bounds rather than one confidence number. The V&V standards supply the product's result-semantics half: V&V 20-2009 (R2021) defines the validation comparison error E = S − D and validation uncertainty u_val = sqrt(u_num² + u_input² + u_D²), keeping numerical, input-parameter, and experimental-measurement uncertainty as separate budget components, and bounds model error by E ± u_val "for a specified variable at a specified validation point" — validation-domain scoping that is the direct doctrinal precedent for the product's `out_of_regime` verdict and its distinct uncertainty kinds.

## insufficiency

No knowledge representation at all: Dakota variables are bare names with no units, no dimensions, no reference frames, no declared physical meaning — nothing prevents feeding pressure into a temperature slot; the model's semantic content lives inside the opaque simulation code Dakota deliberately never inspects. No logic layer, no inference, no proof objects: Dakota cannot state or check a claim, only propagate numbers. No fail-closed vocabulary — the input deck parser checks keyword syntax, not meaning. No result taxonomy: outputs are statistics tables and log text; a solver failure surfaces through ad-hoc failure-capturing conventions (a "FAIL" marker in the results file), not a typed disposition like non_convergent vs backend_unavailable. No provenance receipts: restart files support re-running, but nothing binds source/IR/artifact hashes, solver identities and exact versions, seeds, and tolerances into a replayable certificate. Uncertainty typing is enforced only at input declaration; it is not carried as typed evidence claims attached to conclusions. On the standards side, V&V 20's validation domain is a report-level concept: no machine artifact encodes the domain, so nothing can mechanically refuse a query outside it — an out_of_regime answer today is a sentence a human writes in a PDF. Extension of validation metrics from validation points to an application point is a live research practice (regression/extrapolation papers in the 2025 ASME VVUQ proceedings), not a checked mechanism. Units, regimes, and uncertainty typing across the whole programme are enforced socially — by expert authors, reviewers, and committee reaffirmation — never by a compiler.

## narrowing_history

Dakota did not narrow — it broadened: it began in 1994 as a shared optimization-tool interface for Sandia structural-analysis engineers tired of writing custom couplers, and accreted UQ, calibration, sensitivity, surrogates, and hybrid strategies over three decades, going open source (LGPL) at v5.0. The narrowing happened in the standards: ASME V&V 20-2009 deliberately restricted itself to quantifying accuracy "for a specified variable at a specified validation point" — scalar quantities of interest, point comparisons, with prediction beyond validation points left to interpolation/extrapolation guidance rather than normative procedure — because the committee could standardize an uncertainty-budget arithmetic but not a general theory of predictive credibility; interpretation disputes continue in the literature (e.g. "On the Interpretation and Scope of the V&V 20 Standard," ASME VVS 2020). V&V 10 likewise shipped as a "common language and conceptual framework" (a Guide, retitled Standard only in 2019) with the harder content pushed into companion documents (10.1 worked example, 10.2 uncertainty, 10.3 metrics). The programme's original 1990s–2000s ambition — machine-checkable predictive credibility for simulation (Oberkampf's evidence-theory work aimed squarely at this) — settled into documented human procedure.

## lessons

Copy: (1) the parameter/response black-box contract — Dakota's entire adoption story is that it demands zero modification of the simulation code; the product's solver orchestrator should keep that property, including explicit failure-capturing semantics (retry/recover/abort) which map cleanly onto the product's disposition axis. (2) The variables-block discipline: aleatory and epistemic as structurally different declared types (distributions vs intervals/BPA structures), with mixed propagation defined as nested loops producing interval-valued results — this is a debugged, published semantics (Eldred et al.) the product can adopt wholesale instead of inventing. (3) V&V 20's u_val decomposition as the formal content of a "supported" verdict, and its validation-point scoping as the formal content of out_of_regime. Mistake to avoid: an untyped keyword deck over bare variable names — forty years of this ecosystem proves that units, frames, and regimes left to convention stay unchecked forever. Deeper warning: Dakota won by asking nothing semantic of its users; a fail-closed language asks everything. The product must make the typed layer pay for itself immediately (compile errors that catch real deck mistakes, receipts that satisfy a reviewer) or it will lose to the input-deck culture on friction alone. Also note the LGPL/paid-standard licensing left room a permissive license can occupy.

## cluster_answer

Mechanically, the separation lives in two places today. In Dakota, it is a type distinction in the input deck's variables block: aleatory uncertain variables carry named probability distributions; epistemic uncertain variables are intervals, discrete ranges/sets, or Dempster–Shafer belief structures (interval sets with basic probability assignments). Method composition keeps them separated through the computation: mixed problems run second-order probability — an outer loop sampling/optimizing over epistemic realizations, an inner aleatory probabilistic loop — or interval-valued probability / evidence theory, producing p-boxes and belief-plausibility bounds, never a single collapsed number. In ASME V&V 20, the separation is an uncertainty budget: u_val = sqrt(u_num² + u_input² + u_D²) keeps numerical, input, and experimental uncertainty distinct, and the model-error statement E ± u_val holds only "for a specified variable at a specified validation point" — the validation domain is the set of such points, and extending metrics to an application point is current research (regression-based extension, VVUQ 2025 proceedings), not standardized mechanism. Why no fail-closed typed language emerged: first, the opacity boundary — the semantics (units, frames, constitutive assumptions, regime limits) live inside heterogeneous, often export-controlled simulation codes that Dakota's zero-intrusion contract deliberately never inspects; the very design choice that made adoption possible forecloses compile-time checking of what the numbers mean. Second, the standards' product is a document for human reviewers and regulators — credibility assessment assumes an expert in the loop, so the committees standardized procedure and report structure, not machine-checkable vocabulary; a standard sold as a PDF has no compiler. Third, constituency: the people capable of specifying mechanical checks are the same experts whose judgment the current practice pays for; nobody inside the loop was blocked by its absence. The barrier is therefore workflow culture plus the black-box boundary, not missing formal machinery — the formal pieces (typed uncertainty, uncertainty budgets, domain scoping) all exist and are debugged; what never existed is a user population willing to re-author its models in a checkable language. The product confirms a real gap but must expect that same barrier: its wedge is exactly the models small enough to re-author, where Dakota-scale codes are not the competition.

## largest_unverified_assumption

That nothing in the ecosystem mechanically enforces uncertainty typing, units, or validation-domain scoping — i.e., that enforcement is purely social. This negative is inferred from not finding such a mechanism in Dakota's docs or the standards' descriptions (and from NIST IR 8298's framing of V&V as practice), not from an authoritative statement; internal Sandia QMU tooling or NASA-STD-7009 credibility tooling could contain partial mechanical checks I did not examine.
